Behind on business taxes? What to do in the first week

By Geoffrey Folkerth · November 13, 2026 · 6 min read

YouTube video goes here

Most owners who fall behind on taxes didn't plan to. A slow quarter, a bookkeeper who left, a year when the money went to payroll instead of the estimated payment. Then the first letter comes, and it's easier to put it in a drawer than to open it.

If that's where you are, the good news is that this is one of the most common problems I see, and it has a well-worn path out. In the first week, the order you do things in matters most.

Short version: open every letter, file even if you can't pay, payroll taxes first, and get a pro before you call the IRS.

Day 1: Open everything and make one list

Get every tax notice into one pile: federal, state, payroll, sales tax. Open them all. For each one, write down who sent it, what tax and period it covers, the amount, and any deadline printed on it.

This is usually the hardest step, emotionally. Once the list exists, people almost always tell me it's less frightening than what they'd imagined. You can't plan around a number you haven't looked at.

Days 2–3: Find out what's unfiled, not just unpaid

Not filing and not paying are different problems, and the IRS treats them differently. The failure-to-file penalty is generally 5% of the unpaid tax for each month the return is late (up to 25%). The failure-to-pay penalty is generally 0.5% a month. Filing on time, even when you can't pay, usually saves a lot of money.

So the question to answer this week is which returns haven't been filed at all. Those come first.

Days 3–4: Put payroll taxes at the top of the list

If your business has employees, the money withheld from their paychecks for income tax, Social Security and Medicare isn't yours. You're holding it for them. When it isn't sent in, the IRS can hold owners and other "responsible persons" personally liable through the Trust Fund Recovery Penalty.

That means unpaid payroll taxes can follow you even if the business closes. If you have to choose what to pay first, this is almost always it.

Day 5: Stop the bleeding going forward

Before you fix the past, make sure the hole isn't getting deeper. That usually means:

  • Making current payroll deposits on time, starting now.
  • Setting aside sales tax as you collect it, ideally in a separate account.
  • Putting a rough estimated payment on the calendar for next quarter, even if it's smaller than it should be.

The IRS is generally more willing to work with a business that is staying current on new obligations.

Days 6–7: Bring in the right people before you call anyone

You'll probably need a CPA, enrolled agent or tax attorney to file the back returns and talk to the IRS or state for you. The IRS offers installment agreements for businesses that qualify, and in some cases penalties can be reduced. A good tax professional will know what fits your situation.

Where I come in is everything around that: getting the books accurate so the returns can be filed, building a cash forecast so the payment plan is one you can actually keep, and making sure the same thing doesn't happen next year.

What not to do

  • Don't ignore certified letters. Deadlines in them are real.
  • Don't pay old taxes with money that's needed for current payroll taxes.
  • Don't agree to a payment amount on the phone before you know what you can afford each month.

Being behind on taxes can feel like a personal failing, but it is almost always a cash flow and bookkeeping problem with a known fix. The sooner you start, the less it costs.
